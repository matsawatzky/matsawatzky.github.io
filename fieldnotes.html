Initiative - From Idea to Implementation

October 2026

Engineering Leadership

One of the things I've enjoyed most about working in data is discovering opportunities to improve how something is done.

Sometimes those opportunities are relatively small. A report could be simplified, a repetitive task could be automated, or an existing process could be made a little easier to maintain.

Over the past year, I've had the opportunity to help introduce Python as an additional tool within our organization. What began as a proposal to expand our technical capabilities gradually became a larger effort involving research, presentations, approvals, implementation, and eventually building tools that people could actually use.

It's been a valuable experience, particularly because the technical development was only one piece of the process.

Getting a new capability into an organization involves considerations that don't always appear when you're working on a personal project. Security, maintenance, governance, organizational priorities, and the people who will ultimately depend on the solution all need to be considered.

I've come to appreciate that a good technical idea isn't necessarily a complete solution. It also needs to be practical within the environment where it will operate.

This month, I was fortunate to receive the inaugural Think Forward Award in recognition of this work. It was an unexpected and genuinely meaningful moment, particularly knowing that colleagues had taken the time to nominate me.

But what I've found most rewarding is seeing an idea that once existed primarily as a proposal become something tangible.

There's still plenty to improve, and every new capability seems to introduce another collection of things to learn.

If anything, the experience has reinforced how much I enjoy working at the intersection of technical problem-solving and organizational improvement

And how satisfying it can be when an idea makes its way beyond the planning stage.

Understanding - The Other Side of the Classroom

September 2026

Teaching Learning

This month marked an interesting change of perspective as I returned to the University of Winnipeg, this time as a instructor rather than a student.

It's been a few years since I first learned many of the concepts I'm now introducing to students, and something I've quickly noticed is just how much knowledge becomes automatic with experience.

When you've been programming for a while, you rarely stop to think about why a loop behaves a certain way or what happens when a value is passed into a method. You develop an intuition for these things and eventually stop consciously working through every individual step.

Teaching, however, doesn't give you that luxury.

It's one thing to know that a piece of code works. It's another to explain why it works, particularly to someone encountering the concept for the first time.

Even introductory topics like variable types, conditional statements, and loops contain little details that are easy to overlook once they've become second nature

I've also been reminded that what seems obvious to one person can be genuinely confusing to another. An explanation that makes perfect sense in my head doesn't necessarily translate into one that makes sense to thirty students hearing it for the first time.

Sometimes a small example or a different way of phrasing something makes all the difference.

One of my favourite parts so far has been asking students to predict what a program will do before running it. Beyond making the class more interactive, it's a useful way to see how people are reasoning through the code.

And when the predictions are wrong, those moments often provide some of the best opportunities to explore the underlying concepts.

I'm still figuring out my approach to teaching, but I've already found it rewarding to revisit familiar material from this side of the classroom.

It turns out that explaining the basics isn't always quite as basic as it sounds.

Foundation - Moving Forward by Going Back

August 2026

Learning Mathematics AI

There's something slightly amusing about having spent years studying computer science, working with data professionally, and then finding myself sitting down to review trigonometry.

Yet that's exactly where some of my learning has taken me recently.

As my interests have expanded toward machine learning and AI engineering, I've been spending more time thinking about the knowledge that sits beneath the tools and frameworks we use.

Modern software makes an extraordinary amount of complexity accessible. With relatively little code, it's possible to train models, run statistical analyses, or interact with sophisticated AI systems.

That's incredibly powerful, but it also makes it easy to use something without necessarily understanding much about how it works.

I've been working through mathematics again, with plans to revisit calculus, linear algebra, and statistics in greater depth. Why spend time doing this when it is no longer necessary? I want to develop better intuition for the concepts these tools are built around.

And let me tell you it's been an interesting exercise in patience.

After working in the practical, returning to foundational ideas can feel like coming to a full stop. There's no tangible deliverable, no script to deploy, and often nothing particularly impressive to show for an evening of studying, other than some scribbles in a notebook from working through a problem.

But I've noticed that concepts I once approached primarily as academic exercises feel different when I can connect them to problems I've encountered professionally.

I'm also realizing that understanding doesn't always develop in a straight line. Sometimes revisiting a familiar subject with a few more years of experience reveals connections that weren't obvious the first time around.

For now, I'm enjoying the opportunity to be a student again, even if it means occasionally getting humbled by mathematics I thought I had learned years ago.

Reliability - Working Is Only the Beginning

July 2026

Python Automation Data Pipelines

There's a particular satisfaction that comes from seeing something you've built work for the first time. Those of us who write code know there is not other rush quite like it.

Whether it's a complicated query returning the expected results or a Python script successfully processing thousands of records, that first successful run feels like crossing the finish line.

But I've increasingly come to realize that getting something to work is often just the starting point.

As I've spent more time developing data pipelines and automating processes, the questions I've found myself asking have changed.

What happens if a file is missing? What if a process fails halfway through? How do I know the data is complete? Can the process safely run again without duplicating records? And, perhaps most importantly, can someone other than me understand what's happening?

These questions have pushed me toward concepts like incremental processing, validation, error handling, and that word that developers dread to hear, documentation.

None of these are particularly glamorous additions to a project. In fact, they often involve spending a surprising amount of time thinking about things you hope will never happen.

Yet they're what make the difference between a script that works under the right conditions and a process that can be depended on when conditions aren't quite so ideal.

It's also changed how I think about automation. Saving several hours of manual work is valuable, but reducing uncertainty and eliminating the need for constant oversight can be just as important.

I still enjoy the excitement of getting that first successful run. I just find that my definition of "finished" has become a little more demanding.

And occasionally, a little more frustrating.

Patterns - The Story Between Data Points

June 2026

Analytics Data Interpretation

One of the interesting things about working with data is how much the interpretation can change depending on the timeframe you're looking at.

A single number might tell you where something stands today, but it doesn't necessarily tell you how it got there.

I've been exploring this quite a bit recently while developing analytical features from historical financial data. Rather than focusing exclusively on current balances or activity, I've been looking at how those values behave over time.

Consider two accounts with the same average balance. On the surface, they might appear quite similar. But one could maintain a relatively consistent balance throughout the year, while the other regularly fluctuates between having very little and considerably more.

The average is identical, yet the underlying behaviour is quite different.

Of course, there's a balance to strike. Not every reporting problem requires a new architecture, and sometimes the straightforward solution really is the right one. This is where measures like volatility, rolling averages, and trends become useful. They allow us to describe aspects of behaviour that aren't immediately obvious from a snapshot. Even something as simple as looking at the lowest typical balance over several months can provide a different perspective on how consistently funds are maintained.

What's been particularly interesting is that adding more data doesn't automatically make an analysis more meaningful. The challenge is deciding which measurements actually capture something useful, rather than simply creating more numbers to examine.

A twelve-month trend might help identify a gradual change, while a three-month window might better reflect recent behaviour. Neither is inherently better; they're answering different questions.

I've found myself appreciating this distinction more as I work through feature development.

Data is often presented as though the numbers speak for themselves, but the timeframe, context, and measurements we choose have a significant influence on the story we end up telling.

Perspective - When the Problem Gets Bigger

May 2026

Systems Data Engineering Problem Solving

When working with data, it's easy to become focused on the problem directly in front of you. A report needs to be updated, a query is taking too long, or two sources are producing slightly different results. Naturally, the first instinct is to fix whatever isn't working.

Lately, I've been trying to spend a little more time asking why the problem exists in the first place.

As I've become more involved in building data infrastructure, I've started to recognize how frequently individual reporting challenges are symptoms of larger structural issues. Data might be scattered across several systems, or a process might rely on knowledge that exists only in someone's head.

Any one of these problems can usually be solved individually, but those solutions don't necessarily prevent the same issues from appearing elsewhere.

This has influenced how I approach system design. Rather than focusing entirely on delivering the immediate output, I've been thinking more about how data moves through an organization, where complexity is introduced, and whether a solution could make future work easier.

Of course, there's a balance to strike. Not every reporting problem requires a new architecture, and sometimes the straightforward solution really is the right one. It's surprisingly easy to introduce more complexity while trying to eliminate it.

I think that's been the interesting part of this shift in perspective. I'm spending less time thinking exclusively about whether I can solve a problem and more time considering what kind of solution the problem actually warrants.

Sometimes that means building something new. Other times, it means realizing that a much smaller change will do just fine.

Trust - Knowing When to Throw Data Away

April 2026

Data Analysis Data Engineering

There’s an instinct in data work to keep everything.

More data feels safer. More complete. More “correct.”

But this month pushed back on that idea.

Some fields looked fine at first glance, but the deeper we looked, the more they broke down. Inconsistent, partially filled, or just wrong often enough to create doubt.

At a certain point, the question changed.

Not “can we use this?” but “should we?”

And the answer was no.

Those fields were removed entirely—replaced with empty values, intentionally. It felt strange at first. Like throwing away information. But the result was a system that people could actually trust.

Because bad data doesn’t just sit quietly, it leaks into decisions, dashboards, and assumptions.

And in that sense, removing unreliable data isn’t a loss of information.

It’s an increase in clarity.

Speed - See the Forest, Not Just the Tree

March 2026

ETL Data Engineering

There’s a point in a project where everything works… but something still feels off.

That showed up this month in an ETL pipeline. It was clean, correct, and reliable… but just slow enough to make every run feel a little heavy.

So I stepped back and asked a different question: what is each tool actually good at?

Python is great for orchestration, cleaning, and shaping data. The database is built to ingest and store it efficiently.

That’s when it hit me. I had been so focused on building the pipeline that I lost sight of the system as a whole, and ended up asking one tool to do the job of the other.

Switching to bulk inserts let each part of the system do what it was designed for, and everything changed. Minutes became seconds, and the pipeline went from something that worked to something that felt right.

This was the perfect reminder that: good systems aren’t just built, they’re composed.

When each tool is used for its strengths, performance improves, complexity drops, and the whole system becomes easier to reason about.

Algorithms – The Structure Shapes the Solution

February 2026

Algorithms Data Structures

One of the biggest takeaways from my algorithm design course so far is realizing that most algorithms are not something to be memorized. They emerge naturally from the properties of the data structure being used. When you understand the structure, the algorithm often becomes obvious, as algorithms are often just constraints meeting structure.

Examples:

  1. Choosing the right structure often reduces a complex algorithm to a simple operation repeated efficiently.
  2. Many algorithms succeed because they exploit guarantees such as ordering, hierarchy, or locality.
  3. Good algorithm design is often the art of turning a problem into one that a known structure already solves well.

In that sense, algorithm design feels less like inventing clever tricks and more like discovering the structure already hiding inside the problem.

Programming – Systems Thinking from Biology

December 2025

Systems Design

Coming from kinesiology influenced how I think about software systems.

Biological systems work through interacting subsystems such as:

  1. circulatory system
  2. nervous system
  3. muscular system

Software architectures behave similarly. A good system is not one giant component. It’s a collection of smaller components that communicate clearly.

Healthy systems share traits:

  1. clear boundaries
  2. feedback loops
  3. redundancy
  4. resiliency/adaptability
